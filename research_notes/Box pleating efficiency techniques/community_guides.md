## Q2. Pythagorean stretches (PS / "pytha"), Kamiya patterns, GOPS / GPS: definition, grid construction, triggers, effect

### Takeaway
A pytha lets two flaps whose *square boxes overlap* but whose *circles do not* sit closer than pure 45°/90° BP allows. It works by running the ridge pattern along the bisectors of a Pythagorean right triangle (3-4-5, 5-12-13, …), so every crease still lands on grid points.

On the grid it is a parallelogram plus two "arms". For a 3-4-5 the parallelogram edges have slopes arctan 2 and arctan 3. It is constructible by eye when the overlap box has an even area; otherwise it needs partials, or GOPS from BP Studio.

It is the main lever the community uses to close BP's efficiency gap to circle packing. Abrashi puts that gap at "almost 40%" in extreme cases.

### Cited Findings
**Definition and naming**
- Lang's definition, as quoted on the Origami Forum: a BP technique in which "two diagonally-oriented leaf vertices are allowed to move closer to each other" than square packing would normally permit. This creates axial and ridge creases at angles other than 0/45/90° near the stretch ([Origami Forum "Pythagorean Stretch" thread, 2007](https://snkhan.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?t=3505)).
- Tsai: solutions based on the 3-4-5 triple are very common and "are also known as Kamiya patterns", made famous by Satoshi Kamiya. "Perfect" Pythagorean stretches are rare, and imperfect ones often come with **gusset slivers** ([Tsai, "The story of Box Pleating Studio"](https://origami.abstreamace.com/2021/06/02/the-story-of-box-pleating-studio/)).
- Abrashi also calls the technique "Kamiya's pattern." He describes the element as a group of ridge creases *shared* by two adjacent overlapping polygons; it is not a polygon itself ([Abrashi, "Pythagorean stretch"](https://abrashiorigami.com/pythagorean-stretch/)).
- **Uncertain attribution.** No source I found credits a single inventor. The 2007 forum thread credits Lang with using the technique and lecturing on it, and cites Kamiya's Wasp CP as an example ([forum](https://snkhan.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?t=3505)).

**Why Pythagorean: Wong's 6.1 derivation [ASR]** ([6.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BDiDOsAM44c))
- **Fish base on a square.** Stretching a waterbomb base (a 2×2 BP base with four 1-unit corner flaps) into a fish base lengthens the base. But the flaps become √2 and 2−√2 long, and the elevation becomes irrational, so it cannot mesh with a grid. The cause is the rabbit-eared 45-45-90 triangle's irrational hypotenuse.
- **Fish base on a 3×4 rectangle.** On a 4×4 grid, rabbit-ear the 3-4-5 triangle inside a 3×4 rectangle instead. The base reaches **5 units**, longer than the paper's 4-unit side, which "in normal box pleating would not be possible".
- **Everything comes out rational.** The hypotenuse divides as flap 2 / river 1 / flap 2, and every flap length and the axial+1 elevation are integers.
- **Bisector slopes.** For 3-4-5 they are arctan 2 and arctan 3; slopes ±2, ±1/2 and ±3, ±1/3 all count.
- **Scaling.** Sinking the same structure onto an 8×8 grid doubles everything, giving a 10-unit hypotenuse.
- **Arms.** The "arms" redistribute the hypotenuse, e.g. 4/2/4 → 5/1/4 → 6/0/4.

**Construction rules: Wong's 6.2 "eyeballing" method [ASR]** ([6.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vaNPyzmIRx4))
- **Trigger.** With pure 45/90° creases, flap *squares* may not overlap. With pythas, only the *circles* must not overlap.
- **Worked example: the corner flap grows from 4 to 6.** Fixed neighbours: a 4-unit center flap, a 4-unit edge flap and a 3-unit edge flap. Pure BP fits only a 3- or 4-unit flap in the remaining corner (plus a meander or a 1-unit filler). Pythas allow a **6-unit** flap whose box overlaps a neighbour's in a 2×3 rectangle.
- **The parity test.** Compute the area of the overlap box. **Even area** → the pytha "works out evenly" as a parallelogram plus two arms and can be eyeballed. **Odd area** (e.g. 1 or 3) → it needs partials and odd slopes: wiggle the flaps until the overlap is even, or ask BP Studio.
- **Rule 1: slopes.** The parallelogram's slopes must meet at 45° (acute corners) and 135° (obtuse corners). Use arctan 2 with arctan 3 (from 3-4-5); 5-12-13 gives slopes 2/3 and 1/5. Pairs such as arctan 4 with arctan 2 fail. He estimates 3-4-5 covers "maybe 70–75%" of cases. This is his estimate, not a measurement.
- **Rule 2: obtuse corners.** They lie on the diagonal "tracks" through the corners of the overlap box.
- **Rule 3: acute corners.** They lie on the diagonal ridges from the flap centers.
- **Arms.** Each arm runs from an obtuse corner to the overlap-box corner, then turns out to meet the next flap's ridge.
- **Hinges.** They reflect across the parallelogram's ridges, so flap polygons become concave.
- **Sliding.** A pytha can slide along its diagonal tracks without changing anything.
- **Rivers.** A flap-plus-river can be treated as one big circle when building the pytha.
- **Second example.** The overlap area of 12 failed starting with slope 3 but worked starting with slope 2.

**Abrashi's geometry** ([Abrashi, "Pythagorean stretch"](https://abrashiorigami.com/pythagorean-stretch/))
- Integer right triangles with hypotenuse ≤ 25: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25.
- The angle between the two bisectors adjacent to the hypotenuse is always 135° for any right triangle. Kawasaki then forces the opposite angle to 135°.
- He tabulates nine grid ratio pairs that build a 135° angle, e.g. 1:2–1:3, 2:5–3:7, 1:4–3:5.
- The overlapping rectangle needs at least one even side for the **parallelogram form**; otherwise the **universal form** is needed.
- The element must sit between the two circle centres and cannot pass a centre.
- Overlapping *circles* are illegal, and no stretch fixes that.
- Worked cases:
  - 12×12 polygons with a 2×3 overlap.
  - 14×14 and 8×8 polygons, 1×2 overlap, 2-unit river: the 8×8 becomes 10×10 to absorb the river.
  - 14×14 and 10×10, 1×3 overlap: uses the 2:5–3:7 combination.
  - 14×14 and 8×8 with a 5-unit river: the 8×8 is enlarged to 13×13, which creates a 4×5 overlap and allows a parallelogram.
- Downsides: the CP is more complex and harder to fold, and moving the element leaves unused paper.
- **Advanced topic no. 1.** Sliding the element toward a *non-square* polygon (whose inscribed circle can move inside it) lets the element shrink or vanish. A "classical ridge arrangement" then achieves the same result more simply ([Abrashi, "Pythagorean stretch – advanced topic no.1"](https://abrashiorigami.com/pythagorean-stretch-advanced-topic-no-1/)).
- **Forum example.** A 6-8-10 (scaled 3-4-5) stretch joined overlapping flaps of length 4 and 6 ([forum](https://snkhan.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?t=3505)).

**GOPS / GPS (Tsai & Lang)** ([Tsai, story of BPS](https://origami.abstreamace.com/2021/06/02/the-story-of-box-pleating-studio/))
- GOPS (Generalized Offset Pythagorean Stretch) allows an **offset greater than 1, with the extra offset sunk back into the layout**. That gives "a much larger solution space", so a valid solution is more likely.
- Because GOPS is integral, multiple gadgets combine into compound solutions. Tsai generalized the construction to layouts of 3+ flaps.
- Lang's GPS (originally "graphical", later "generalized") is GOPS's complement.
- In both, **the area of the overlap rectangle** is key, which led to a construction based on **integer factorization**. Relay patterns are built by cutting overlap regions.
- Tsai notes that Meguro, Murakami, Kamiya and Imai had earlier used essentially equivalent patterns.
- Papers: Lang & Tsai, "Generalized Offset Pythagorean Stretches in Box-Pleated Uniaxial Bases," *7th OSME*, Vol. 2, 2018, pp. 591–606; Tsai, "On the Constructions of Generalized Offset Pythagorean Stretch Patterns," *8th OSME*, Vol. 4, 2025, pp. 3–19 ([BP Studio Notes/bibliography](https://bp-studio.github.io/notes.html); [Springer chapter](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-96-6711-6_1)).
- **BP Studio support.** It searches for stretch patterns for flaps whose rectangles overlap but whose circles do not. As of v0.8 it covers all valid 2-flap layouts, most valid 3-flap layouts and many 4-flap patterns; unsolved flaps get "?" marks ([BP Studio manual](https://bp-studio.github.io/manual.html)). Its homepage says these gadgets let BP reach efficiency "nearly as good as the optimal circle packing" ([BP Studio home](https://bp-studio.github.io/)).
- A scorpion design walkthrough used "simple GOPS" via BP Studio. It noted that overlap configurations for more than two rectangles, and some half-integral cases, were not yet implemented at the time ([cExplr scorpion](https://cexplr.github.io/blog/origami-scorpion-design-from-start-to-finish)).

**Measured effect in a real design.** In Wong's lesson 5.5, pulling the leg flaps together with a pytha shrank the human-figure packing from a **36 grid to a 32 grid** [ASR] ([5.5](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0uPTFBkuJ4E)). That is an 11% linear reduction, so a model about 12.5% larger for the same paper. The percentage is my arithmetic.

**Same triangle, other uses.** Wong points out that the arctan-2 triangle in his on-grid ridge level shifter is the same triangle that appears inside a 3-4-5 pytha. The same slopes are those of the arctan-3 tilted grid [ASR] ([5.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PPyQmFRZKjw)).

### Inferences
- **When to use a stretch rather than a plain hinge.** A pytha is needed exactly when two flap boxes (or a flap box and a river band) overlap while the Euclidean circle constraint is still satisfied. If boxes merely touch, plain 45/90° hinges and ridges suffice.
- **Parity tests.** Wong's "even overlap area" and Abrashi's "at least one even side" are the same condition for integer rectangles. An overlap box with an even area must have an even side, and vice versa. That gives a cheap test for whether a 3-4-5 parallelogram pytha exists. Odd areas need universal or offset forms, i.e. GOPS.
- **Gain for two flaps.** Lengths a and b meeting diagonally need L∞ separation a+b in pure BP, but only Euclidean separation a+b with a pytha. For the 3-4-5 case the centres can be (3k, 4k) apart instead of (5k, 5k), and the hypotenuse 5k equals the summed flap and river lengths. Wong's 4×4-grid fish base that is 5 units long is the minimal demonstration.

### Gaps
- I found no source that measures average grid savings from pythas across a corpus. The "almost 40%" figure is Abrashi's extreme-case BP-vs-circle-packing gap, not a pytha saving.
- I could not read the full Lang–Tsai GOPS paper or Tsai's 2025 paper (paywalled or not indexed), so the exact GOPS parameterization (offset, factorization of the overlap area) is from Tsai's blog summary only.
- The phrase "Kamiya stretch" (as opposed to "Kamiya pattern") was not found in any source.

## Q3. Level shifters: what "level" means, how they are built, and what they buy

### Takeaway
In axial (uniaxial) BP, a crease's **level or elevation** is its height above the axis in grid units. Without shifters, valleys sit at axial (0) and mountains at axial+1, which makes flaps one unit thick; this is why BP models look like stick figures. A **level shifter** transitions an axial-family crease to another elevation, making a region wider (or thinner) so the model reads as 3-D rather than a stick figure.

There are two families:
- **Ridge level shifters:** squash a ridge, then sink. Continuous range axial−1 to axial+2, and no host needed.
- **Pleat ("general") level shifters:** a perpendicular pleat hosts the transition. Each pleat buys up to ±2 levels, and pleats stack.

Wong & Demaine (OSME 2024) proved the exact condition for a ridge transition between arbitrary pleat sets: an alternating-sum and parity condition.

### Cited Findings
**Vocabulary [ASR]** ([5.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v4ZJzLWAK10))
- In axial BP every hinge is perpendicular to the axis in the folded form.
- "Axial+1" and "axial+2" describe *creases*; "axial" and "unaxial" describe *crease patterns*.
- Stone Golem: valleys at axial, mountains at axial+1, then +2 and +4 regions. The "diamond" structures are +1 transitions and the "heart" structures are +2 transitions.

**Ridge level shifters [ASR]** ([5.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PPyQmFRZKjw))
- Demo on a waterbomb base sunk into thirds (four 3-unit flaps). Thinning one flap by sinking it alone causes a "train wreck" where its new axial crease meets the neighbour's axial+1 crease.
- The fix is to squash the ridge and sink through, which transitions axial → axial+1 cleanly. Squashing "up" instead lifts axial → axial+2, which makes flaps *thicker*.
- **Pros:** works on any existing base at any ridge, with no special setup; the height is continuous (axial+1/3, +1/6, +1.5 …); can go below the axis (axial−1, −1/2), which also gives a color change.
- **Cons:** practical only up to axial+2. Higher needs very long flaps and complex structures.
- **On-grid version.** The naive squash creates 22.5° creases, which "will never fully lie on a grid" because tan 22.5° is irrational. Wong replaces it with a triangle of slope 2 (arctan 2) whose vertices are all grid points.
- Example: Lang's turtle uses these shifters in the corners so the fins are about 2 units wide instead of being stick-figure flaps.

**Pleat ("general") level shifters [ASR]** ([5.3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XSZpbx-yy4s))
- Wong calls this "the much more common" type. It requires a **pleat perpendicular to the axial pleats**.
- **Mountain/valley rule:** the mountain of that pleat goes on the side of the *lower* elevation.
- **Variants from one 1-unit pleat:** (a) inside-reverse-fold, popped up to axial+2 with a sloped edge; (b) a push-through version reaching axial+2 with a sharp corner, usually preferred; (c) two adjacent +2 lifts with the pleat between them lifted again, reaching **axial+3** from one pleat.
- **"+2 shifter" naming:** a +2 shifter is two +1 shifters with the strip between them lifted; it moves axial+1 to axial+3. "Think of the structures not the names."
- **Stacking:** "every pleat will give you plus two more away from axial plus one". One horizontal pleat gives at most ±2; two pleats give ±4 (e.g. axial+5 back to axial+1); three give axial+7 (from axial+1, at 2 per pleat).
- **Hidden layers can be shifted too:** the layers behind the front pleat can be lifted, and pleats can be repositioned in the back.
- All exercises are on a **16×16 grid**. He calls pure level shifting "basically what unaxial box pleating is," and says his regular-grid bison translation is "one step away from tilted grid."
- **Example:** a "potato" model is just two horizontal pleats, one axial+4 shifter and "a lot of stretch" for the leaves.

**Incorporating shifters into a packing [ASR]** ([5.4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pNagY0LUGls))
- **One-unit flaps as pleats.** A 1-unit center flap or edge flap can be collapsed either as a free flap or as a locked pleat. A pleat can then be level-shifted.
- **Reading the tree.** The shifter pattern's tree is read from its side view as a sequence of 1-unit flaps and rivers, e.g. 3-unit river, two 1-unit flaps, 2-unit river, two 1-unit flaps, 3-unit river.
- **Width.** The 16-grid exercise needs **8 units of width**, so the hosting center or edge flaps must be ≥8 units long. Extra pleats on the side "doesn't hurt."
- **Packing the host.** Pack those long center or edge flaps, bend the rivers to remove side gaps (flaps grow a bit), then "slap" the shifter pattern over the pleats.
- The pentagon-shaped regions cannot be trimmed into; anything else can.
- The Stone Golem (18 grid) uses long edge flaps as pleats for the arms and long center flaps for the legs.

**Abrashi's level shifters** ([Abrashi, "Level shifters"](https://abrashiorigami.com/level-shifters/))
- Level shifters "allow us to change the elevation of axial creases" and can make flaps "twice as wide."
- **45° ridge shifters:** the two new creases at the vertex must form 135° (Kawasaki). There are infinitely many solutions; choose the collision points that stay on the grid. They lose no paper and need no dummy element.
- **Shifters at horizontal or vertical ridges:** these lose paper and "always need some kind of host", a dummy element that is invisible in the final model.
- **Size costs:** an asymmetric shifter needs about "two basic units" to reach its elevation, a mirrored one three, and a symmetric one "a little bit less than two and a half". Higher elevations need longer flaps ("at least 6 units" in one example).
- **Hidden vs visible:** a hidden shifter lost **4 units of paper and piled 5 layers**; a visible version reached the same elevation with **3 layers**, preserving "twice as much paper."
- **Universal shifters:** the defining line must be a closed contour, with the first and last elevations matching on both sides.

**Theory (Wong & Demaine, OSME 2024)** ([paper PDF](https://erikdemaine.org/papers/PleatTransitions_OSME2024/paper.pdf), [paper page](https://erikdemaine.org/papers/PleatTransitions_OSME2024/))
- **Motivation:** "Level shifters are often used to create figures that are not a single uniform width — for example, insects with both thin legs and a wide shell, or human figures with both detailed hands and feet but also wide clothing."
- **Prior art:** ridge level shifters are "well known" to shift axial+1 → +2 and other integer heights (citing Abrashi 21).
- **Theorem.** For two sets of parallel pleat rays meeting a ridge at angle θ, with x-intercepts a₁<…<aₘ and b₁<…<bₙ, a locally flat-foldable transition exists **iff a₁−a₂+a₃−… = b₁−b₂+b₃−… and m ≡ n (mod 2)**. Necessity follows from generalized Kawasaki.
- The two pleat sets must also have the *same* angle θ.
- **Algorithm:** a constructive algorithm builds the transition.
- **Application:** it **terminates "dense bouncing"** in uniaxial bases. Pick a transition ridge, bounce axial creases until they hit it, and build a transition there. The authors conjecture this always works when a closed axial polygon bounds the ridges.
- Dense bouncing is "usually solved by discretizing ridge lengths to a grid system such as box pleating or hex pleating."

**Wong's own use.** In one model, "along the boundary between the normal 40ths and the partial 80ths is a nice wall of level shifters." This enables "quite extreme variation in crease density," so one region can be far more detailed and smaller than the rest ([Wong, "An Origami Journey"](https://web.mit.edu/wongb/www/origami/gallery/journey.html)).

**Tool support.** BP Studio does not implement elevations or half-integral structures ([BP Studio manual](https://bp-studio.github.io/manual.html)). Wong calls level shifters "a pocket of unaxiality in the otherwise completely axial CP (that's why BP Studio doesn't do level shifters)" [slides] ([TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)). He also lists "cannot express partials and transitions, in other words level shifters" as a BP Studio con [ASR] ([crash course](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OCDR8cRvKAY)).

### Inferences
- For a pipeline whose bases are stick figures, level shifters are the community's standard lever for *legibility*: wide shells, clothing, torsos. In the tree they cost extra 1-unit flaps and rivers that host the pleats, roughly 2 units of hypotenuse or flap length per shift (Abrashi), and they add layers unless built visibly.
- The Wong–Demaine alternating-sum condition is a mechanical feasibility check for any ridge transition. It suits automation better than the hand recipes.

### Gaps
- I could not see the on-screen crease patterns for 5.3's homework shapes, so exact grid layouts of the +1/+2/+3 shifters (heart and diamond) are described from narration only.
- I found no source that quantifies how much hidden-layer bulk a level shifter adds in general, beyond Abrashi's single 5-layer vs 3-layer example.

## Q4. Hinge shifts, offsets, grid parity (odd/even) and half-grid ("partial") offsets

### Takeaway
I found little explicit community teaching under the names "hinge shift" or "grid parity." The relevant practice appears instead as:
- **Partials / half-integral units:** sub-grid pleats such as Wong's "partial 80ths" inside a 40 grid, reached through a wall of level shifters.
- **Odd-area overlaps** that force partials in pythas.
- A strong community preference for **even, ideally power-of-two grids.**

Hinge placement in packing→CP has several valid solutions with different thickness.

### Cited Findings
- **Partials in Wong's work.** He describes a CP on "normal 40ths" with regions folded on "partial 80ths" (a half-grid), separated by a wall of level shifters ([Wong, Journey](https://web.mit.edu/wongb/www/origami/gallery/journey.html)).
- **Partials in pythas.** Odd overlap-box areas need "partials and slightly more complicated structures" [ASR] ([6.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vaNPyzmIRx4)).
- **Tool support.** BP Studio does not implement "half-integral unit structures"; its workarounds are widening flaps, using integral structures, and stub flaps for meandering rivers ([BP Studio manual](https://bp-studio.github.io/manual.html)). The scorpion author found "some half-integral cases" of GOPS unimplemented ([cExplr](https://cexplr.github.io/blog/origami-scorpion-design-from-start-to-finish)).
- **Grid-size convention.** In an r/origami thread, an experienced reply says odd grids (31/33/35) are unusual. It advises starting from an even grid, "preferably a square, cube or power of two", unless the design needs a center unit or no line of symmetry ([r/origami mirror, Jan 2025](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/origami/comments/1hydjiz/nice_grid_size_fitting)). This is anecdotal and seen only through a third-party mirror.
- **Hinge choices.** When converting a packing to a CP, missing hinges at flagged vertices can be resolved in several valid ways, "which is more work, gets thicker"; Wong drags them "the closest way to the edge" [ASR] ([4.3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5pMxh01Dnqc)).
- **Ease of grid folding.** Abrashi compares grid sizes by constructibility: power-of-two grids are easiest via repeated halving ([Abrashi category page](https://abrashiorigami.com/category/box-pleating/), post "Dividing paper into thirds, fifths, sevenths…").
- **Choosing 16 over 15.** Wong picks 16 over the minimal 15 partly because it "is also a little bit easier to fold from" [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)).

### Inferences
- Parity in the community appears mainly in three places: odd-area overlap boxes, which force universal or offset pythas or partials; the even-grid and book-symmetry preference, where an even N puts the symmetry axis on a grid line; and half-unit partials isolated by level shifters.
- A pipeline that mirrors pairs about x = N/2 inherits the same preference for even N.

### Gaps
- No community source I accessed defines "hinge shift", "shifted hinge" or "hinge offset" as a named technique. These may be Lang *Origami Design Secrets* terms (not accessed here) or Discord vernacular.
- A rigorous treatment of odd/even grid parity was not found.

## Q5. Rivers: routing, bends, meanders, gap filling, and river-centric stretches

### Takeaway
A river is a constant-width band that separates the tree's two sides. It must run edge to edge or close on itself, and it needs a ridge diagonal at every bend. Designers use bends and meanders to fill empty space, and can move "thickness" from flap pleats into the river. A flap plus a surrounding river can be treated as one big circle when building a pytha.

### Cited Findings
- **Rules [ASR + slides]:**
  - Rivers must run from edge to edge or loop back on themselves.
  - A river must keep the same width all the way as it meanders.
  - There is a ridge crease at every bend.
  - Every unit of paper must belong to a flap or a river ("no unfilled gaps").
  - The river runs perpendicular to the pleats.
  - Flaps on one side of a river end up on one side of the tree.
  - Sources: [4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8), [lesson 4 slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1iD7jaEIcR1WsLbMzQ1G06g-b0CCU3WViHhZDzVOXCxA/edit), [4.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PzkAL2Dng20).
- **Gap filling [ASR]** ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)):
  - Fill gaps by meandering the river or by adding extra pleats to a flap.
  - For color-change designs, "instead of making the flap take up the space you make the river take up the space" by bending rivers. This avoids pleats on an edge flap.
  - In 5.4, bending rivers downward compacts the packing; the flaps grow a little, which "that's okay, we're okay with extra pleats" [ASR] ([5.4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pNagY0LUGls)).
- **Tool checks.** BP Studio infers river courses and flags rivers that cover more area than needed as "unnatural rivers" (blue stripes, implying meandering). Uncovered regions show yellow, and invalid overlaps show red ([BP Studio manual](https://bp-studio.github.io/manual.html)).
- **Rivers in pythas** [ASR] ([6.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vaNPyzmIRx4)): when a flap's box overlaps a river band, "think of the river like it's going around the flap… one big circle." Solve the pytha for that combined circle, then copy it back; locally it is identical.
- **Abrashi's river cases** ([Abrashi PS](https://abrashiorigami.com/pythagorean-stretch/)): rivers absorbed by enlarging a polygon (8×8 → 10×10 for a 2-unit river; 8×8 → 13×13 for a 5-unit river, making a 4×5 overlap). Or the river is first reconfigured to surround a single polygon.
- **Edge River Method** [slides] ([TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)): a method for unaxial and tilted-grid polygon maps, explained to Wong by Mu-Tsun Tsai.
  - Abstraction edges become rivers: internal edges run polygon → polygon, external edges run polygon → paper edge. This relaxes axial BP's edge-to-edge rule.
  - "We can use pythagorean stretches to overlap our rivers when they bend."
  - Wong calls ERM "more of a theoretical thing", used when negative space cannot otherwise be removed.
- **Central fold opening** ([Abrashi, design process part 2](https://abrashiorigami.com/origami-design-process-part-2/)): it adds a second river, which shortens the legs, lengthens the body and hides three small flaps inside.

### Inferences
- A river's cost in perimeter is 2 × length, the same as a flap of that length (see Q8). Meanders and bends are the community's standard way to spend slack grid area without inventing new flaps.

### Gaps
- No source here names "corridors", "side flaps" or "shallow rivers" as distinct techniques. These terms may come from Lang's book or BP Studio's internals.

## Q6. Flap placement: corner vs edge vs center (middle) flaps, elevation, and their cost

### Takeaway
The community rule is to **put the biggest flaps in corners, then edges, and use center flaps only when needed**, typically when the minimum grid reaches about 20. The reasons:
- A corner flap of length L uses an L×L box. The same flap on an edge uses about twice the area: Wong's 4-unit example, 16 vs 32 cells.
- Center flaps are "hardly ever free" in the collapsed base and are harder to collapse.

### Cited Findings
- **Corner vs edge cost [ASR]** ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)): a 4-unit corner flap takes a 4×4 = **16** cell box, but the same 4-unit flap on an edge takes about **2×16 = 32**, "half the paper" in his 8-grid sketch. That leaves less room and a thicker model. Start with the biggest flaps and prioritize corners. This is "just a general rule of thumb."
- **Packing rules [ASR + slides]** ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8), [slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1iD7jaEIcR1WsLbMzQ1G06g-b0CCU3WViHhZDzVOXCxA/edit)):
  - The *center* of every flap must lie inside the square.
  - Flaps may not overlap except with pythas.
  - Pleats can be added to all three flap types (corner, edge, center).
  - Keep the cyclic order of flaps around the edge consistent with the tree. "you cannot twist the order"; TreeMaker enforces this.
- **When center flaps are needed [ASR]** ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)): "center flaps make everything weird." Small trees usually avoid them; when the minimum grid size gets to "around 20 or higher… you're probably going to end up with some center flaps." Example homework: a squid with a 4-unit head as a center flap.
- **Center flaps are trapped** ([Abrashi, "Can central flaps be free?"](https://abrashiorigami.com/can-central-flaps-be-free/)):
  - A central flap is usually trapped between the axial lines of an adjacent flap. It rolls into a neighbour only across a symmetric hinge whose central axial line is a valley.
  - **At most one central flap can be free** in a configuration.
  - Freeing it means opening the axis, which removes the central axial line and **doubles the base width**.
  - Central flaps are harder to collapse than edge flaps.
  - The Little Warrior design omits the central crease joining its two central flaps and opens it during collapse ([Abrashi design process part 2](https://abrashiorigami.com/origami-design-process-part-2/)).
- **Edge and center flaps as level-shifter hosts.** One-unit (or wide) center and edge flaps can be collapsed as locked pleats that host level shifters (5.4, above) [ASR] ([5.4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pNagY0LUGls)).
- **Elias stretch** (collapsing edge flaps) ([Abrashi, "Elias stretch"](https://abrashiorigami.com/elias-stretch/)):
  - A manoeuvre for assembling flaps (polygons) that sit on the paper edge, "named after Neal Elias, who popularized it in the 1970s." It is "universal" regardless of the number and shape of edge polygons.
  - Procedure: accordion-collapse the model; pick an edge-to-edge ridge; start at the ridge point farthest from the edge; stretch row by row toward the edge.
  - Work from the centre outward, because working from the edge "put[s] too much stress on the paper."
  - Other sources date Elias's BP development to the 1960s and describe a series of inverse shovel folds performing an Elias stretch step by step ([Shovel Folding, 6OSME](https://origamimuseum.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/ShovelFolding6OSME-04.pdf)). The 1960s-vs-1970s dating conflicts between sources.
- **"Elevation" in the level-shifter sense** is covered in Q3. BP Studio does not model elevations ([manual](https://bp-studio.github.io/manual.html)).

### Inferences
- **Area costs by position.** For a flap of length L, the box area is about L² in a corner, about 2L² on an edge (a 2L×L strip) and about 4L² in the center (a 2L×2L box). The perimeter "edge-unit" cost is 2L in all three cases (Q8).
- **The trade-off.** Corners are cheapest in *area*; the center is cheapest in nothing except where the anatomy demands it (heads, tails on the axis). Each center flap also risks being locked and doubles base width if freed. This matches Wong's corner-first heuristic.

### Gaps
- I found no source with a precise rule for when a center flap must be used beyond Wong's "minimum grid about 20" rule of thumb.

## Q7. Beyond plain BP: 22.5° hybrids, unaxial (non-uniaxial) BP, tilted grid

### Takeaway
The community distinguishes:
- **axial (uniaxial) BP:** tree plus packing, the kind BP Studio supports;
- **unaxial / non-uniaxial BP:** grid-based, but no tree, flaps or rivers; it uses polygons. Examples are tessellations, Lang's cuckoo clock, Kato's bison and tilted grid.

**Tilted grid** is unaxial BP on a grid tilted to slope 3 (about 18.4°). It is usually diagonally symmetric. It is more efficient and more organic for wide-bodied subjects, but locks pose, is poor for spiky subjects and is hard to fold.

Mixing 22.5° with BP is generally discouraged on-grid, because tan 22.5° is irrational. Hybrids nonetheless exist, e.g. Brian Chan's Newt, labelled "box-pleat + 22.5."

### Cited Findings
- **Taxonomy [ASR + slides]** ([5.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v4ZJzLWAK10), [TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)):
  - BP = on a grid with most creases at 90° or 45° to each other.
  - Axial BP uses tree, flaps, rivers and hinges; within it, uni-, multi- and cross-axial variants exist, but "you can assume it's uniaxial."
  - Unaxial BP is everything else: Moser's train, Lang's cuckoo clock and organist, many tessellations.
  - Tilted grid "is unaxial (which is the much less common version of box pleating)."
- **Jason Ku's guest lecture** (MIT 6.849, 2010): box pleating is characterized by "only multiples of 45 degrees" as opposed to the 22.5° system. Some of his BP models are not uniaxial bases "but they're still limited by this stretchability constraint" ([MIT 6.849 lecture material, search summary](https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.849/fall12/lectures/L05_images.pdf)). I relied on the search summary and did not read the slides directly.
- **Why slope 3 for tilted grid** [ASR + slides] ([TG video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rnsL1Vz7y20), [slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)):
  - Tilt by a *slope*, not an angle; irrational slopes (e.g. 22.5°, 30°) never meet grid points.
  - For the same square, the diagonal symmetry line crosses grid points **5 times at slope 2, 7 at slope 3, 2 at slope 4**.
  - Slope 3 and slope 1/2 are exactly 45° apart, so the center line hits many grid points and the CP is easier to pre-crease and collapse.
  - "arctan 3 is the most convenient even if at the expense of some efficiency."
  - Pre-crease references come from Shuki Kato's book.
- **Tilted grid method** [slides] ([TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)):
  - Draw an "abstraction": overlapping convex polygons for limbs and muscles, i.e. the unshaped base.
  - Pack the polygons; joints are overlaps.
  - Remove negative space using Lang's distance constraint: "it turns out the cp will need about 2 units more distance."
  - Slice to a half-square (a 1×2 rectangle or an isosceles right triangle) containing all polygons, tilt it to shrink it, then reflect it to a square.
  - Practical version: start on an arctan-3 grid and fit polygons into the triangle.
- **Negative-space example** [slides + ASR]:
  - Two points are 6 units apart on the abstraction and meet at a concave joint, so "you have to add one unit" and they need 7.
  - The diagonal distance 5√2 ≈ **7.07** barely suffices with a pytha. On-grid without a pytha, only horizontal or vertical distance counts (5).
  - Fix: pull the polygons apart by 2 units, making it 5+2 = 7.
  - The kangaroo uses this extra-pleat trick between its legs to avoid "webbed" limbs.
- **Pros and cons of tilted grid** [slides]:
  - **Pros:** efficient; good for organic shapes; little explored.
  - **Cons:** limited shaping freedom ("the back leg is pretty much completely locked", with no hinges to flap); unsuitable subjects ("things with a lot of negative space… thin limbs", e.g. insects, where axial BP is more efficient); hard to fold and design.
  - One subject (a bodybuilder dummy) was done on a normal, a diagonal and a tilted grid. The tilted version was most efficient, "but not by much," and much harder to fold. The tilted grid's sliced edges also look more organic.
  - Rough heuristic: tilted grid suits triangle-shaped subjects (kangaroo), normal grid suits rectangular ones (bison) [ASR].
- **22.5° in BP** [ASR] ([5.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PPyQmFRZKjw)): "we don't like mixing 22 and a half with box pleated," because its slope is irrational and never lands on the grid however fine the grid. Use arctan-2 triangles instead.
- **Hybrids that exist:**
  - Brian Chan's Newt (2005) is described as "box-pleat + 22.5," following Kamiya and Jason Ku CPs; the 22.5° system simplifies the folding sequence ([MIT Newt page](https://www.mit.edu/~chosetec/origami/newt)).
  - A search snippet also mentioned Tim Rickman's "octopleating", using near-22.5° angles to soften inefficient BP features, from an OrigamiUSA listing. **Unverified**: I did not open that page.
- **Lesson 4.3 aside** [ASR] ([4.3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5pMxh01Dnqc)): 22.5° designs use irrational references (e.g. 1/√2) found by angle bisectors rather than rational fractions; Wong says he does little 22.5°.
- **Squash/sink for width:** the ridge-squash level shifter (Q3) is the on-grid squash/sink conversion for flap width.

### Inferences
- In this community, "non-uniaxial BP" and "BP with tilted flaps" mostly mean polygon-packing / unaxial BP and tilted grid. They trade flap mobility (posability) for efficiency and broad surfaces. For a pipeline built on stick-figure trees, that is a different design paradigm rather than an incremental gadget.

### Gaps
- I found no community guide on "BP with 22.5 gadgets" as a systematic method, beyond individual hybrid models.

## Q8. Efficiency metrics and grid-size heuristics

### Takeaway
- **Efficiency** is the ratio of folded-model size to starting-sheet size.
- **Grid-size heuristic.** The community's starting point is Wong's perimeter bound, **N ≥ 2·Σ(tree edge lengths)/4**, optionally minus center-flap lengths. Round up by 1 or more for slack, then to a comfortable even number.
- **Gap to circle packing.** BP is less efficient than circle packing, by up to about 40% in extreme cases. Pythas and GOPS recover much of that; BP Studio claims "nearly as good as" optimal circle packing.
- **Automatic packing.** TreeMaker's circle optimizer plus BP Studio's rounding is the de facto automatic packer.

### Cited Findings
- **Definition** [ASR + slides] ([6.1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BDiDOsAM44c), [TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)): "efficiency is the ratio between final model size compared to the initial starting paper." A huge sheet folded into a small, thick model is inefficient. Wong notes efficiency "is not the only thing that matters", also folding sequence and annoyance.
- **Grid-size bound** [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)). Wong says this is not in Lang's book.
  - **Bound:** grid size N ≥ (sum of tree lengths × 2) / 4, counting every flap and river edge.
  - **Proof idea:** a flap or river of length L consumes 2L units of the paper's perimeter; an N-grid square has 4N perimeter units.
  - **Example:** tree total 30 → 60 edge units → N ≥ 15. He chose **16**, which gave "4 units of edge to spare"; the packing then spent 2 of those on a 3-unit flap that used 8 perimeter units instead of 6.
  - **Exception:** subtract center-flap lengths, since they don't consume perimeter.
- **Abrashi's efficiency claims** ([Abrashi PS](https://abrashiorigami.com/pythagorean-stretch/)): BP models are smaller than circle-packed ones, by "almost 40%" in extreme cases. BP requires circle centres on grid points, which leaves an unavoidable gap between circles; the Pythagorean stretch largely addresses this.
- **BP Studio's claim** ([BP Studio home](https://bp-studio.github.io/), [repo](https://github.com/MuTsunTsai/box-pleating-studio)): stretching gadgets let BP efficiency get "nearly as good as the optimal circle packing." The app has a TreeMaker-like "Layout optimization" that "is purely numerical and does not optimize aesthetics" ([manual](https://bp-studio.github.io/manual.html)).
- **TreeMaker → BP Studio pipeline** [ASR] ([crash course](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OCDR8cRvKAY)):
  - Save the TreeMaker file (.tmd5) with integer flap lengths and import it into BP Studio. "takes the coordinate of every flap… optimal position, but BP Studio will then approximate it onto the nearest grid point… and a few other twists." "You don't even have to pick the grid size."
  - Origin: Wong's buggy Python "circle pleater" (circle packing → BP), which Tsai then implemented in BP Studio within weeks.
  - TreeMaker "a lot of the time… won't build your crease pattern" for complex trees, but the BP conversion "can fold it 100% of the time."
- **Corner-first packing** saves area (Q6) [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)).
- **Measured grid reduction from a pytha:** 36 → 32 grid (Q2) [ASR] ([5.5](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0uPTFBkuJ4E)).
- **Grids in practice:**
  - Wong's exercise and homework grids are 8, 16 and 32. The Stone Golem is 18; a big model uses 40ths with partial 80ths ([5.4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pNagY0LUGls), [Journey](https://web.mit.edu/wongb/www/origami/gallery/journey.html)).
  - Tsai's Blue Crab BPS uses a 22-unit grid ([Tsai blog](https://origami.abstreamace.com/2021/06/02/the-story-of-box-pleating-studio/)).
  - Common community grids: 16, 24, 48; odd grids such as 43 or 59 are discouraged ([r/origami mirror](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/origami/comments/1hydjiz/nice_grid_size_fitting)).
- **Gap cost of empty area.** A normal-grid translation of a tilted-grid puppy has "a lot of blank spaces everywhere, that's what makes it inefficient" [ASR] ([TG video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rnsL1Vz7y20)).

### Inferences
- **Wong's bound is a perimeter (edge-unit) argument.** It is the BP analogue of the circle-packing perimeter constraint. It is a lower bound only: rivers that bend, gaps and center-flap geometry push N above it. His 30 → 16 example shows about 7% slack chosen up front.
- **Efficiency as a pipeline metric.** For a fixed tree, efficiency is roughly proportional to 1/N, since the model's scale is about (longest path in grid units)/N. So "lowest grid N that packs" is the community's operational efficiency target, matching how BP Studio and TreeMaker are used.

### Gaps
- I found no community-wide benchmark of efficiency, e.g. a standard "flap length / grid" score across models. Efficiency is discussed qualitatively or per model.
- The exact rounding "twists" BP Studio applies on TreeMaker import are not documented in the sources I read.

## Q9. Model quality: thinning, color change, gaps, locks, layers, collapse difficulty, symmetry, bloat

### Takeaway
Community guidance on quality:
- Give thin parts as little area as possible, because area becomes thickness.
- Use visible rather than hidden level shifters to avoid piled layers.
- Prefer wider polygons that can be thinned in shaping over thin ones that cannot be thickened.
- Put color change on paper edges, or below the axis.
- Accept that center flaps lock and are harder to collapse.
- Pre-crease ridges only; collapse from the centre outward (the Elias stretch).
- Book or diagonal symmetry is the norm.

### Cited Findings
- **Thickness from area.** "Larger circles mean thicker flaps, so the tail was given as little paper area as possible." Pure BP without level shifters keeps a narrow subject thin. All paper must be used, and unused paper must be hidden ([cExplr scorpion](https://cexplr.github.io/blog/origami-scorpion-design-from-start-to-finish)).
- **Edge vs corner for the biggest flap.** An edge placement of a big flap makes the model "thicker" (Q6) [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)).
- **Layers.** A hidden level shifter piled **5 layers** and lost 4 units of paper; a visible one reached the same elevation with **3 layers** and "twice as much paper" ([Abrashi, Level shifters](https://abrashiorigami.com/level-shifters/)).
- **Unshaping rule** [ASR + slides] ([TG video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rnsL1Vz7y20)): a leg can be thinned at the feet but "very hard to thicken… for the waist." So design legs 2 units thick all the way down, design straight arms, and give the tail a wide polygon: "a wide tail can be thinned but it's hard for a thin tail to be thickened."
- **Color change:**
  - It "can be realized only on the paper edges." The boundary must be traced as a continuous line on the paper edge, satisfying the perimeter theorem.
  - Example: a letter "P" on a 7×5 card needed 12×12 paper from its 48-unit boundary, then **13×13** to place a corner at a turning point.
  - Incompleteness arises when the paper-to-model ratio is small.
  - Source: [Abrashi, Colour change part 1](https://abrashiorigami.com/colour-change-part-1/).
  - Ridge level shifters taken below the axis (axial−1) give a color change [ASR] ([5.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PPyQmFRZKjw)).
  - Moving the thickness into a bent river instead of edge-flap pleats is "nice for color change designs" [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)).
- **Locks and posability.** Center flaps are locked except at most one (Abrashi). Tilted-grid polygons have no hinges, so poses are locked ([Abrashi, central flaps](https://abrashiorigami.com/can-central-flaps-be-free/); [TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)).
- **Collapse practice:**
  - **Wong:** pre-crease only the ridges; leave top pleats unfolded while collapsing the more complex part, then collapse the rest. For test folds, pre-crease grids "through the layers" to save time [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)).
  - **Abrashi:** the Elias stretch, working from the centre outward ([Elias stretch](https://abrashiorigami.com/elias-stretch/)).
  - **Scorpion walkthrough:** the first CP failed by self-intersection, and layering had to be found by physical folding ([cExplr](https://cexplr.github.io/blog/origami-scorpion-design-from-start-to-finish)).
- **Tool checks.** Orihime/Oriedita's flat-foldability tool flags vertices with odd crease counts and shows the needed angle, e.g. a 3:4 slope inside a pytha [ASR] ([6.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vaNPyzmIRx4)). Its fold preview can reveal "self intersection" from bad hinges [ASR] ([4.3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5pMxh01Dnqc)).
- **Symmetry:**
  - The scorpion was made book-symmetric ([cExplr](https://cexplr.github.io/blog/origami-scorpion-design-from-start-to-finish)).
  - Tilted-grid models are usually diagonally symmetric, and the half-square is reflected "to make the model look good from both sides" [slides] ([TG slides](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/16M6jhZK4MUuGqAG5kYMUfz8ZF6cS7LhbnMdILSzndSo/edit)).
  - For homework trees, Wong draws trees from the top view so packers place legs symmetrically [ASR] ([4.2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nuLFd4Hzoc8)).
- **Pytha downside.** It brings more complex CPs that are harder to fold ([Abrashi PS](https://abrashiorigami.com/pythagorean-stretch/)), and imperfect stretches leave gusset slivers ([Tsai blog](https://origami.abstreamace.com/2021/06/02/the-story-of-box-pleating-studio/)).
- **Hardness context.** "Box Pleating is Hard" (Ku et al.) shows that flat-foldability of box-pleated crease patterns is computationally hard ([BOXPLEATINGHARD LNCS 2016 PDF](https://scripts.mit.edu/~jasonku/pdf/BOXPLEATINGHARD_LNCS2016.pdf)). This is from the title and listing; I did not read the paper here.

### Inferences
- The community treats **area as thickness**. Every unit of paper becomes a layer somewhere, so efficiency (small N) and slimness pull in the same direction: fewer surplus pleats, fewer hidden shifters.
- The quality tools are those listed above: corner-first placement, visible shifters, river-absorbed slack, and pythas that avoid enlarging N.
- "Bloated" models, in Wong's and Abrashi's framing, come from hidden layers and edge placements of big flaps.

### Gaps
- No source quantified "bloat" or layer counts systematically. Locks are discussed only for center flaps and tilted grid.
- Closing gaps (sinks, closed-sink locks) and thinning sequences were not covered in detail by any accessed guide.
- The Discord and Instagram CPs that Wong references could not be inspected.
